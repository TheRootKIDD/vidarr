# Review: "The big-DC MoE LLM design"

Reviewed 2026-09-02 against the 19-point note. Purpose: decide what to build, what to simulate, and what to push back on.

Status labels:
- **Established** — validated in published work at meaningful scale.
- **Plausible** — sound reasoning, no public validation at scale.
- **Unclear** — under-specified or not derivable from the note as written.
- **Overstated** — direction is fine; strength of the claim isn't supported.
- **Opinion** — economics/geography; not testable by this project.

## 1. Summary verdict

The note is a hardware–model co-design, and its strongest idea is the organizing principle: **size the expert to the smallest hardware unit that runs at full utilization, make every expert that size, and build the fabric so that the unit — not the chip — is the addressable node.** Almost everything else (repeated middle layer, continuous scheduling, huge expert pools, one installation per DC, tiny HBM) follows from wanting that unit busy at all times. Read this way it is coherent, and several pieces already have literature behind them.

Main weaknesses:
1. It is an *inference* design. The author confirms (Q3, 2026-09-02) that training is conventional lockstep and that continuous execution is inference-only, which removes the largest gap; what the note still does not say is how variable depth is trained (ACT is the standard answer) and how attention with a sub-linear index is trained — the author calls the latter a research project of its own (Q9).
2. The **router** — top-k over ~10⁵ experts at every one of ~128 iterations, depth-aware, balanced in space *and time*, tenant-masked — is the crux and is not specified.
3. Its **expert granularity** (one coarse expert per 64-chip unit) runs against current evidence that finer experts are better per FLOP.
4. Some latency claims are asserted rather than derived (P2). P12's is now derived (author's Q1 plus the roofline in §P12): it halves the latency *floor* at full utilisation, with half the tokens in flight per unit, at unchanged throughput.
5. The **minimum scale** at which the design beats conventional serving is very large, so nearly every systems claim can only be simulated here.
6. Some "nobody does this" / "silly" statements are out of date: MTP trained from scratch, chunked prefill, multi-LoRA serving and rail-optimized fabrics all exist.

**v2 of the note (2026-09-02)** changes three points: P4 makes the unit width a parameter and names the trade-off (a wider unit halves clos bandwidth but enlarges the failure domain, the minimum expert size and the torus); P5 lists more local-fabric options; P7 adds an attention/KV structure — per-iteration KV seen only locally, one *final-vector* KV per token seen globally — which is the mechanism behind the KV claims in P7, P15 and P16 and is new enough to be treated as its own design point (§P7, `docs/01 §4.3`). Everything else is word-identical to v1.

None of this makes the project uninteresting. The *combination* of a repeated all-expert middle block, two latent streams, depth-adaptive compute and hardware-quantized experts has not been studied together, and each piece is testable at small scale.

## 2. Pros

1. **Expert = hardware unit** turns the granularity debate into a measurable quantity (utilization at batch b) and makes fabric cost per node independent of expert count.
2. **The fabric (P5) is sound and buildable today.** A fat local domain of 64–72 chips plus one clos "rail" per chip position is how NVL72-class domains with rail-optimized InfiniBand/Ethernet are built. The novelty is *usage*: node = expert group with tensor parallelism inside the node, instead of spreading small experts one-per-chip.
3. **Repeated middle layer with the whole expert pool (P6)** composes naturally with variable depth (P7) and continuous execution (P11); the "you can simulate the layered model" argument is right for the FF path (with caveats, §4).
4. **Two-stream token state (P12)** cleanly separates "what I mean to others" from "what comes next", makes prefill cheaper by construction and gives MTP heads (P13) a natural home.
5. **Managed aggregation (P14)** is the chunked-prefill side of a real debate and fits a design where all units are identical.
6. **Multicast, in-network reduction and co-activation placement (P9)** are real levers that current MoE deployments underuse.
7. **Per-vector adapters + bespoke experts on a shared spine (P18)** is a good product idea and mostly engineering, given existing multi-LoRA kernels.
8. The note is honest about being untested and asks readers to have a reason for rejecting each point — that is the project's method.

## 3. Cons and gaps (cross-cutting)

1. **Training story is conventional, by the author's account (Q3): lockstep synchronous training; the continuous scheduler (P11) exists only at inference.** What remains unspecified: training variable depth (P7 — ACT-style ponder cost, straight-through routing), training with the index in the loop (P15 — the author defers it as a separate research project, Q9), and sparse expert gradients (with 10⁵ experts each sees few tokens per step). Q10 settles P17's training: the entailment-to-vector map is learned, so the table is a parametric memory, not a database round-trip.
2. **Router unspecified.** Product-key / hierarchical routing exists for large pools; balancing a *continuous* system over time (all tokens want "early" experts at early iterations) does not.
3. **Granularity conflict.** Fine-grained MoE scaling-law work says smaller experts win per FLOP. The note's experts are deliberately coarse. Either tensor-parallel coarse experts recover this through the larger pool available at every iteration, or the unit should host many small experts (expert-parallel inside the unit). Same fabric traffic either way — so this is the first thing to measure (H4).
4. **Latency accounting.** 128 sequential fabric round trips per token remain. Parallel branches (P2) and MTP (P13) reduce it; "halve" claims are upper bounds (P2) or rest on an unstated baseline (P12).
5. **Minimum viable scale.** Keeping N_e experts busy at b_min tokens per step needs ≈ N_e·b_min/k tokens in flight per iteration. For 10⁵ experts, b_min = 256, k = 8: ~3 M tokens in flight. Below that the design under-utilizes. There is no small deployment of this system, only simulations.
6. **Failure domains.** A tensor-parallel 64-wide expert means one chip failure removes every expert on that unit. v2 P4 acknowledges this as the cost of a wider unit; replication is implied (P9) but not designed; in-flight tokens need recompute semantics.
7. **19 coupled changes.** Attribution is impossible without a strict ablation order (fixed in `docs/03`).
8. **Tone vs evidence.** "Silly" appears where the field has open trade-offs (serial vs parallel blocks, disaggregation vs chunked prefill, HBM). Treat as priors, not conclusions.

## 4. Point by point

### P1 Systolic arrays, not LPUs — *Established (economics), with a caveat*
GPUs/TPUs give the best dense-matmul throughput per dollar and watt; SRAM-resident low-batch designs buy latency with capital cost. Caveat: this design's premise is that batch is always available; any regime where it isn't (small tenants, off-peak, early deployment, agentic workloads that pay for tail latency) is exactly where the other designs win. Nothing to train; encode as a cost-model assumption.

### P2 Parallel attention + FF — *Established*
The parallel block (GPT-J, PaLM, Falcon) trains ~15% faster at scale with negligible loss at ≥60B in PaLM's ablations and a small loss at ~8B. "Halve decode latency" holds only if attention and FF wall-clock are equal and fully overlapped; per-iteration latency becomes max(attn, FF) + dispatch, so it is an upper bound. In this design the two branches run on different hardware (attention owner vs expert units), which is what makes the overlap real. Pitfall: the FF no longer sees this iteration's attention output; with a repeated block it sees last iteration's, and with 2-layer experts (P8) the interleaving gets coarser. Ladder step L2.

### P3 2:4 sparsity from the start; FP4 — *Plausible, high risk*
Hardware 2:4 sparsity accelerates one operand (weights). "Train dense for 1% of tokens, then fix a 50% mask" is an early-bird lottery ticket; evidence for masks found that early at LLM pretraining scale is thin, and a fixed mask removes plasticity. The author's intent (Q7): weights only, mask fixed after the dense warm-up. So the experiment is fixed-early-mask at 2× width vs dense at matched FLOPs; the RigL-style comparison is dropped from the ladder. Compounding FP4 + 2:4 + a weight-tied recurrence is a numerics risk: do sparsity and precision as separate ladder steps (L10a/b). FP4 *pretraining* recipes exist (NVFP4, 2025) but need care (block scaling, stochastic rounding, high-precision layers). Check whether the target hardware's FP4 datapath supports sparsity at all.

### P4 Expert size = smallest FF that fills U chips — *Plausible; central; conflicts with fine-grained evidence*
**v2 adds**: the width may be 32, 64, 128 or anything else; going wider halves the clos bandwidth requirement but enlarges the failure domain, the minimum expert size and the torus. That is ADR-001 in the author's words, and it makes $U$ a sweep axis in S3/S9 (clos bytes vs failure domain vs minimum expert size).
Sizing rule (derived in `02 §2`): tensor-parallel over U chips needs the per-chip slice d_ff/U ≥ s_min (tile width) and a token batch b ≥ b_min = (chip FLOP/byte ratio × bytes/param)/2 to be compute-bound. With d = 8192, U = 64, s_min ∈ [128, 512]: d_ff ∈ [8k, 32k], i.e. 0.2–0.8 B params per expert (SwiGLU). That is a coarse expert. The alternative that keeps the fabric argument intact is expert-parallel *inside* the unit (U small experts, one per chip): identical clos traffic, lower per-chip batch, finer granularity. Which is better per FLOP at fixed unit count is the project's first real question (H4, L7). The note's "will never process fewer weights than this size at a time" is a scheduling invariant kept either way.

**Disagreement added 2026-09-11 — uniform expert width may not survive the quality evidence.** P4 is the note's *organizing rule*: every expert is one unit wide, which is what gives P5's fabric, P9's placement and P16's memory budget a uniform object to schedule. Two verified results now push against it from opposite directions. **FlexMoRE** (arXiv:2602.08818, `05 §P18`) makes experts *rank-heterogeneous* — some full-sized, some low-rank adapters on a shared base — and beats a full-sized mixture 47.18 vs 45.46 on average at **under a third the parameters**, with the optimal rank *substantially higher for reasoning-heavy than for knowledge-heavy* domains. **FlexMoE** (arXiv:2606.27866, `05 §P3`) shows the redundancy is there to exploit even post hoc: ≈ 99.8 % of base performance at 50 % of routed-expert parameters pruned on Qwen2-57B-A14B, *without* fine-tuning. Together they say a uniform width is leaving quality-per-parameter on the table, and that how much is left depends on what the expert is *for*.

The project's question is therefore not "adopt heterogeneous rank" but **"does heterogeneous rank buy enough to be worth giving up the uniform expert?"** — and it is genuinely two questions, one per tier, which must both be answered before the trade can be priced:
- **Quality (Tier A, L7c).** Rank-heterogeneous experts vs uniform-width experts at matched FLOPs and matched total parameters, on top of L5. Runs beside L7's $g$-sweep, which is already the granularity experiment; L7c makes the widths *unequal* where L7 makes them *smaller*. One `screen` run, ≈ 6 h.
- **Systems (Tier S, S11).** What non-uniform experts cost the fabric. A uniform expert is what makes `02 §2`'s tile floor, `02 §4`'s placement and `02 §8`'s residency budget tractable; unequal experts mean unequal unit occupancy, so the scheduler must bin-pack rather than tile, and co-activation placement (P9) loses its symmetry. **This is the half that decides it**, because the quality half is already suggestive and the systems half is the note's actual claim.

**If L7c shows a material quality gain and S11 shows a tolerable fabric cost, P4 as stated is wrong and the note's organizing rule needs restating** — most likely as "a small number of discrete widths" rather than one. That would be the largest single change to the note this project has proposed, so both halves need to be run before it is written down. Recorded here rather than as an ADR because it is a disagreement with the note, not a decision (CLAUDE.md: disagreements go in `00` or `04`). Bears on H4, H18; touches P5, P9, P16.

### P5 N × 64 fabric — *Established (topology) / Plausible (usage)*
**v2 adds**: the local fabric can be a fast router, an asymmetric-bandwidth 4×4×4 torus, an even-bandwidth 3-D torus, a 64-wide 1-D torus, an 8×8 torus or a doubled-bandwidth 8×8 mesh — i.e. the unit fabric is a scenario variable (`02 §1`), and the author's claim is that MoE dispatch is unaffected by the choice.
This is a rail-optimized clos over fat nodes. Verified properties: per-rail bandwidth is 1/U of the node's; a node's aggregate clos bandwidth equals one full rail set; per-port cost is that of a small port. Things to model: multi-tier clos latency (two traversals per iteration), oversubscription, the "asymmetric-bw 4×4×4 torus" inside the unit (Q8: relative bandwidths 1, 4, 16, 64 — read as one per hierarchy level chip → 4-group → 16-group → unit, which lets all-reduce run at full speed with an adapted algorithm; not applicable to GPUs, which use whatever the vendor's fabric gives), and the all-gather/reduce per expert step inside the unit. "No MoE complications" is true for *dispatch*; it is not true for placement, replication and failure (P9, §3.6).

### P6 Few early/final layers + one repeated middle layer with all experts — *Plausible; well precedented; two caveats*
Precedents: Universal Transformer, ALBERT (cost of sharing), MoEUT (UT + MoE with fixes for norms and grouping), recurrent-depth models (2025), Relaxed Recursive Transformers (sharing + per-layer LoRA), HRM-style recurrent modules. Caveat 1: the "simulate the layered model" argument requires the router to *know the iteration index*. The author's view (Q2) is that the router can track depth from the state itself and an explicit index is optional; we keep the depth embedding and ablate the router without it (E-Q2). Without any depth signal a token would pick the same experts at every iteration. Caveat 2: the argument covers FF experts only; attention weights are shared across iterations, so per-layer attention cannot be simulated unless attention also gets per-iteration parameters (per-iteration LoRA is the cheap fix). Also: sharing weights does not shrink the KV cache — the author confirms one KV cache per iteration (Q2), 128 at full scale, fewer locally. Sharing K/V across iterations is an optimisation to test (L6b), not the design. Ladder L5.

### P7 Variable iteration count; RL for compute allocation — *Plausible; training risk* — **v2 adds the KV structure**
**v2's addition, in our words**: per-iteration KV caches are attended only *locally*; *global* (long-range) attention sees one KV per token, built from its final middle-layer vector. Local attention cannot use neighbours' final vectors — that would force token $t-1$ to finish all iterations before token $t$ starts, killing prefill/training parallelism (and speculative decode) — but far tokens are in earlier batches and are already complete, so global attention over final vectors costs no serialisation. Result: the persistent KV cache is one entry per token instead of $r_{max}$, "almost the full 128×" smaller, at the price that batches of the same document run sequentially and a "super parallel" prefill would run in a weaker mode. Our reading: this is Transformer-XL-style segment recurrence with a single global cache (closest precedents: Transformer-XL, Memorizing Transformers, Block-Recurrent Transformers, YOCO/CLA for one global KV), and it changes the spec in four places — attention (`01 §4.3`), the index (it lives over the final-vector cache, per sequence, shared by all iterations — half of Q9), KV tiers and memory budget (`02 §7–8`), and training (segment-sequential per document, `02 §13`). The 128× is exact by construction; the open question is what global attention over final vectors only costs in quality (H15a, ladder L5d — cheap to test, needs no index). Two ambiguities go back to the author (Q11, Q12).
Adaptive depth has precedent (ACT, Mixture-of-Depths, early exit/CALM, recurrent-depth models evaluated at different recurrence counts). Two hard parts the note skips: (i) a token that halted at iteration r_s < j has no state at iteration j for later tokens to attend to — the author's answer (Q5): they attend to the last computed vector, i.e. state copy; with shared attention weights its K/V for every later iteration are one and the same entry, so "variable depth cuts KV cache" holds; (ii) learning the halting policy: ACT-style ponder cost first, RL only as a fine-tuning stage — pretraining-scale RL over discrete compute decisions is high-variance and tends to collapse to "always max" or "always min". "Reduce KV cache significantly" follows from (i) once attention weights are shared across iterations (per-iteration attention adapters, L5b, would break the equality). Ladder L6.

### P8 Experts with ≥ 2 internal layers — *Plausible*
Halves fabric bytes per FLOP. Cost: coarser interleaving of attention and FF. Cheap ablation (L7b). Interacts with P2.

### P9 Multicast + co-activation placement — *Plausible; needs a simulator*
Up-traffic dedup (one token copy up, fan-out at the lowest common switch) and in-network reduction of the k expert outputs on the way back (SHARP-style weighted sum) are real. Placement is dynamic graph partitioning + replication (DeepSeek's EPLB is a static precedent); co-activation statistics drift during training and differ per tenant. The up/down asymmetry claim depends on where attention owners sit — make it an *output* of the simulator (S3), not an assumption.

### P10 One combined installation per DC — *Plausible (statistics) / Opinion (operations)*
Statistical multiplexing is correct: the tokens-in-flight needed to keep experts busy (§3.5) only exist in one big pool. Against: blast radius, upgrades, data residency (EU customers cannot be served from a single non-EU pool), single-model lock-in (partly addressed by P18). Model as a utilization-vs-pool-size curve (S4).

### P11 Continuous, non-lockstep execution — *Plausible; the largest engineering unknown*
Per-expert queues with (min batch, max wait); expert-choice routing is the natural algorithmic partner. Risks: tail latency, per-sequence ordering constraints at attention, deadlock without timeouts, and the fact that today's EP runtimes are barrier-based all-to-all — this is a µs-scale dataflow/actor runtime nobody has shipped at this granularity. Little's law makes latency and tokens-in-flight one budget; model it (S1, S2). Training still needs lockstep or bounded-staleness semantics (§3.1).

### P12 Two latent streams per token — *Plausible (structure) / Established (latency mechanism, after Q1) / Unclear (quality premise)*
Closest precedent: XLNet's two-stream attention (content stream vs query stream). Our best reading of the claims: compare at **equal decode FLOPs per token**. A single-stream model with 2d² FLOPs-scale has width √2·d and 2d² parameters; the two-stream model at width d pushes two vectors through d² parameters. Hence: half the parameters → half the weight bytes per decode step → half the decode latency *in the bandwidth-bound regime*; prefill runs one stream → half the FLOPs. The author's own account (Q1): each vector is half the work, the two run in the same batch, so a step takes half as long; each token occupies two batch slots, so a unit serves half as many tokens at once and throughput is unchanged — the tokens in flight get their answers twice as fast, and with twice the hardware everyone does. The two readings coincide at the utilisation floor: the floor $b_{min}$ is measured in *rows*, a two-stream token is two rows, so a unit at the floor holds $b_{min}/2$ tokens and its step time — equal to the weight-load time there — halves. Above the floor the latency is the same as a single-stream model's at equal token batch; below it the halving is the bandwidth-bound one. Net: two streams halve the *minimum latency reachable at full utilisation*, for the weight-streaming part of the step; fabric round-trips per iteration do not halve (S1). The author adds that it is a nice but non-standard idea and easier to leave out — so it is optional here (L4, last). Remaining caveats: (i) the quality premise — two d-vectors through shared weights matching one √2·d-vector at equal FLOPs — is untested (H12); (ii) the context stream is never directly supervised; it receives gradient only through the prediction stream.

### P13 Multi-token prediction trained from the start — *Established*
MTP from scratch improves sample efficiency at scale and enables self-speculative decoding (Meta 2024; DeepSeek-V3 ships it with one extra depth and reports ~85–90% acceptance). Four heads is aggressive — acceptance decays per head. Caveat specific to this design: speculation saves *latency*, not FLOPs; a throughput-bound batched system gains less, and verifying 4 draft tokens is a variable-depth pass here. Reading heads off the prediction stream only is consistent with P12. Ladder L3.

### P14 No prefill/decode disaggregation; managed aggregation — *Plausible; overstated*
This is chunked prefill (Sarathi-Serve) generalized to a per-unit prefill:decode ratio. It removes KV transfer and SLO interference. Disaggregation's *other* reasons — different parallelism configs and different hardware for the two phases — vanish only because this design makes all units identical and HBM-light. So the claim holds inside the design's assumptions, not in general. Tension: P12 creates exactly the "prefill-only encoder" the author says would justify disaggregation. Simulate both (S5).

### P15 Indexed attention, cold KV on SSD — *Plausible; constants dominate*
Sparse/indexed attention has a large literature (Memorizing Transformers, Unlimiformer, Landmark, Quest, MagicPIG, RetrievalAttention, NSA, DeepSeek's "lightning indexer"). What the note asks for beyond it: a sub-linear structure over the keys (n = the number of keys the query could attend to; the author's "index" is this structure, not a scoring module — Q9) instead of O(n) scoring, and training with that structure in the loop. The author considers this a research project in itself and has not designed it; the design in `docs/01 §6` (shared per-iteration indexer, DSA-style) is ours. After v2 the index lives over the final-vector cache: one structure per sequence, shared by all iterations and heads, holding one entry per token — which answers half of Q9 and makes the SSD claim rest on a cache $r_{max}$× smaller than we assumed. Pitfalls: (i) query count — per generated token, iterations × heads queries against that one index unless one low-dimensional indexer per iteration is shared across heads and selects *blocks*; (ii) incremental index maintenance per decoded token; (iii) SSD bandwidth works only if cold hits are rare — that needs a measured locality model, not a hope; (iv) exact copy/induction over far context degrades when the index misses — keep a dense local window. The search-engine analogy has a flaw the author would appreciate: search runs ~1 query per request; attention runs thousands per token. Ladder L9 (quality) + S6 (bandwidth).

### P16 ~4 GB HBM per chip, or no HBM — *Plausible for inference in this design / Overstated in general*
Inside this design, expert units are compute-bound by construction (batched tokens, thin per-chip weight slices), so per-chip memory need is capacity for resident experts (~256 GB per 64-chip unit holds hundreds of FP4 experts) plus a hot KV window. The argument works *here*. It does not transfer to (a) training, where weights + grads + optimizer state ≈ 16 B/param and capacity is the binding constraint; (b) dense or long-context serving with dense attention; (c) the knowledge table (P17), which is capacity-hungry. "Global run on DRAM for no reason" is opinion. No commodity accelerator ships with 4 GB, so this is advice to chip designers, not a saving available to us. Cost model only (S7).

### P17 Per-iteration knowledge retrieval from a vector DB — *Plausible; precedents exist*
This is a memory layer (product-key memory; Memory Layers at Scale; PEER) whose values are *populated from a knowledge pipeline* rather than learned from scratch, plus RETRO's "don't spend weights on facts". Architecturally it is one more expert reached over the same fabric. The author's design (Q10): run a corpus (all of Wikipedia) through an entailment pipeline, turn each entailment into one or more vectors, and *learn* the entailment-to-vector map during training — or start from a pretrained embedding and update it by backpropagating into it (preferred if the table will be extended later) or by adding a layer in front of it; the table can be refreshed but need not be; entailment extraction itself he calls a research project. His addendum: one can skip entailments and use sentence embeddings directly. So the values are parametric, $v_n = g_\theta(\mathrm{enc}(s_n))$ over corpus sentences, which is what makes the table extensible (ADR-009); the trade is that a sentence carries several facts plus coreference noise while an entailment is one deduplicated fact — worth revisiting (L8d) only once the table is shown to be used at all. Pitfalls: entailment extraction at scale is expensive and noisy; the model may learn to ignore a noisy table; poisoning and versioning become model-quality issues. Ladder L8 with a clean, small source.

### P18 Per-vector tiny QLoRAs; bespoke experts; shared spine — *Plausible; mostly engineering*
Multi-LoRA serving with per-token adapter gather exists (Punica/S-LoRA kernels). Specific to this design: an adapter on the *shared* middle block applies ~128 times per token — cheap and powerful, possibly unstable. Bespoke experts need a tenant mask in the router. Isolation: shared batches leak timing; regulated tenants may refuse. Ladder L11 + S8.

### P19 One giant DC, arctic or orbital — *Opinion*
The latency point is fair for most workloads; data residency, blast radius, power procurement and politics are why nobody does it. Record, don't test.
